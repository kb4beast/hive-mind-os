## Part 0 — Read this before you pitch anything

### The audience change, and what it costs you

The tournaments optimised for an Expanding Frontiers grant panel. **Investors score a
different rubric**, and three of your best grant arguments become liabilities in front
of them:

| Grant panel rewards | Investor asks instead |
|---|---|
| Regional economic impact, RGV jobs | "Does the market exist outside Brownsville?" |
| NASA technology basis | "Do you own anything, or are you licensing what anyone can license?" |
| Non-dilutive capital efficiency | "What does this look like at $50M of revenue?" |
| Community benefit | "Who signs the cheque, and how often?" |

So each deck below is rebuilt around the four questions an investor actually asks:
**who pays, how much, how often, and why can't someone else do it.** The regional
material stays, but demoted to a "why we win here first" slide rather than the thesis.

### The rule that governs all four decks

**Say the weak thing first.** Every one of these ideas has one genuine hole, named in
its deck. An investor who finds a hole you did not disclose stops trusting everything
else in the deck, including the parts that are true. An investor who watches you name
your own hole and show the plan to close it is evaluating a founder, which is what they
are actually doing anyway.

Boom Baseline's hole is that no payer is confirmed. Peninsula's is that it looks like
consulting. SARGO's is arsenic. The Boom Map's is that it is a high school project.
None is fatal. All four are fatal if concealed.

### Numbers discipline

Every figure in these decks is graded. **A** = primary or corroborated. **B** = single
credible source. **C** = weak, do not put on a slide. **DERIVED** = arithmetic on other
figures, with the assumption stated out loud.

If you cannot say which grade a number is, take it off the slide.

# Deck 1 — Boom Baseline

> **The measurement layer every airport has and no spaceport does.**

## Elevator pitch

**One line:** Every major airport continuously measures its own noise. Not one
spaceport does. We build that layer, starting at the busiest launch site in America.

**Thirty seconds:** Airport noise monitoring is a $343 million a year market because
airports learned decades ago that you cannot grow operations a community does not
trust. Spaceports never built it. Now the FAA projects launch and reentry operations
going from about 330 a year to over 1,000 by 2030 and 10,000 by 2035, and the first
launch community in America has taken its operator to federal court. We deploy
calibrated, independently operated overpressure and acoustic monitoring around launch
sites and sell the record to everyone who currently has to argue without one: the
operator, the spaceport authority, the regulator, the insurer and the county.

## Why it helps, and who it helps

This is the slide that separates you from a grievance project. **The record helps the
operator most.**

| Who | What they have today | What the record gives them |
|---|---|---|
| Launch operator | Predictions that were wrong by 1 to 4 psf on one measured flight (S22), and a lawsuit | A defensible, third-party number; the ability to prove a mitigation worked |
| Spaceport authority | Anecdote | The instrument every airport authority already runs (S23) |
| FAA | Models with a measured miss (S22), and a mandate to license 30x more operations (S32) | Ground truth to calibrate against |
| Insurer / county | No basis to price or adjudicate | Actuarial data |
| Residents | A courtroom | A number |

Airports did not solve community noise by winning arguments. They solved it by
measuring, publishing, and making the number the shared reference. That is the
business.

## The deck, slide by slide

**1. Title.** Boom Baseline. *The independent measurement layer for launch communities.*
One image: a launch, and a house, in the same frame.

**2. The problem.** Launch cadence in populated areas is now limited by public consent,
and consent is being negotiated with no shared instrument. Around 80 plaintiffs across
roughly 60-70 households in Port Isabel and South Padre Island are in federal court
over eleven tests between April 2023 and October 2025 (S21, grade A). Nobody in that
dispute, including the operator, has a continuous calibrated record.

**3. Why now.** Three things became true at once, and none was true five years ago:
- FAA projects over 1,000 licensed launches and reentries a year by 2030 and 10,000 by 2035, from about 329 worldwide in 2025 (S32, grade A/B). That is roughly a 30x increase.
- The FAA opened rulemaking on **siting new spaceports** in August 2026 (S33, grade B). More launch sites near more people is now policy, not speculation.
- The first community-versus-operator case is live (S21), so the cost of having no number just became visible to everyone.

**4. The insight.** *Spaceports are where airports were before noise monitoring was
standard.* Toronto Pearson runs 25 permanent IEC 61672 Class 1 terminals streaming
continuously (S23, grade A). Envirosuite alone serves over 200 environmentally
constrained airports (S30, grade B). At spaceports this market is $0.

**5. The product.** A network of calibrated overpressure and acoustic nodes, an
independently held chain-of-custody record, and a published method anyone can attack.
We measure **what arrived**. We never claim what it caused. That restraint is the
product: a number both sides can use is worth more than a number one side likes.

**6. Market.** Presented as a ladder, with the honest grade on each rung:

| Rung | Figure | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Analogous market today: airport noise monitoring systems | $342.5M (2025), $366.9M (2026), $579.8M (2034) | B |
| Wider environmental noise monitoring | $2.4B (2025) to $4.6B (2034), 7.5% CAGR | B |
| Launch sites globally | 80 orbital and suborbital | B |
| FAA-licensed US spaceports | 14 (9 horizontal, 4 vertical) | A |
| Spaceport community monitoring today | **$0** | A |
| Operations growth to 2035 | ~329/yr (2025) to a projected 10,000/yr | A/B |

**Say this out loud:** "I am not going to divide a market figure by a site count and
call it a TAM. The honest statement is that the analogous market is $343 million a
year, the asset class is 80 sites growing under a federal siting rulemaking, the
operations that drive the need are projected to grow 30x, and the current spend is
zero. Price discovery is the first thing your money buys."

**7. Business model.** Per-site annual monitoring subscription plus an installation
fee, with a data licence for insurers and authorities. Recurring, infrastructure-shaped,
and it grows with cadence rather than with headcount.

**8. Traction.** Be exact about what exists and what does not. Today: the analysis, the
site relationships, and a costed deployment plan. In 90 days: a calibrated reference
network capturing real events. There is a cheaper proof running in parallel, described
in Deck 4, which produces a real dataset for about $2,000.

**9. Moat.** Not the sensors, which are commodity. Three things: the **longitudinal
calibrated record**, which cannot be back-filled by a competitor arriving later; the
**independence**, which the operator structurally cannot self-supply for a dispute it
is party to; and the **method**, published and defended in public until it becomes the
reference.

**10. The ask.** $750K pre-seed for 18 months: reference network at one site, first
published record, price discovery, and two paid pilots. Alternative on the same slide:
a $250K design-partner contract, which we prefer, because a customer is better than a
cheque.

**11. Use of funds.** Roughly: 40% deployment and calibration, 25% engineering, 20%
commercial and price discovery, 15% legal, consent and data policy.

## Anticipated questions

**"Who actually pays? Name them."** *The honest answer, delivered first:* not yet
confirmed, and it is the first thing this round resolves. The candidate payers are the
operator, the spaceport authority, the county, insurers and the FAA. The airport
analogy says the operator or the authority pays, because that is who pays at airports.
I will not tell you I have a signed customer when I do not. What I will tell you is that
30 days of price discovery is the first milestone and you can hold me to it.

**"Won't SpaceX just publish their own data and kill you?"** They may, and it would be
a real competitive event. It does not settle the dispute, for the same reason airports
do not self-certify community noise: the value is independence, and an operator's own
number is not usable by a party in dispute with the operator. Also note what happened
when the prediction was tested: measured overpressures exceeded FAA predictions by one
to four psf (S22). Self-reported modelling is exactly what lost credibility.

**"This is a lawsuit support business."** No, and I will not build one. Same data, same
terms, to every party including the operator. The moment the record is for sale to one
side, its value collapses to zero, because the other side stops accepting it. That is a
commercial argument, not an ethical pose.

**"Sensors are commodity. What stops a competitor?"** Correct, and the sensor is not
the product. The product is a calibrated chain-of-custody record with a published
method and a longitudinal series. A competitor starting in 2028 cannot produce 2026 data.

**"Is this venture scale or a lifestyle business?"** Honest answer: unproven, and it
depends entirely on whether per-site contract values look like airport contracts. If
they do, 80 sites growing under a federal siting rulemaking is a real business. If they
do not, this is a public-good programme and you should not fund it. That is a
falsifiable question and it is milestone one.

**"Why you?"** Because the work is already specified, the site relationships are local,
and the cheapest possible proof is already designed and running at student scale for
about $2,000. Ask me again after the reference network is up.

**"What if the FAA mandates it and a big incumbent takes it?"** That is the good
outcome for the market and the dangerous one for us, so we sequence for it: be the
published method and the incumbent record before a mandate lands. Envirosuite got there
in aviation by being early, not by being biggest.

**"Regulatory risk?"** The primary risk is the opposite of the usual one: we need the
FAA to care more, not less. The August 2026 siting rulemaking (S33) points that way.
Verify its current status before you rely on it.

**"Privacy exposure from measuring private homes?"** Real, and handled before any
deployment: written consent per site, publication aggregated by street or larger, no
address ever identifiable. This is a live workstream, not a slide.

## Kill criteria

Walk away if: someone is already doing calibrated community monitoring at a spaceport;
or no payer of any kind will state a number after 60 days of trying; or the only
willing payer is a litigation funder.

# Deck 2 — Peninsula

> **Two industries that have never been neighbours are now neighbours, and nobody owns the risk between them.**

## Elevator pitch

**One line:** A $12 billion LNG export terminal and a methane-fuelled orbital launch
site now share one peninsula, and no model exists for what one does to the other.

**Thirty seconds:** Process safety is a mature discipline with mature vendors, and every
one of them models a facility against its own hazards and its own fence line. Nobody
models a facility against a *neighbouring facility of an entirely different industry*
with a different regulator, because until now that pairing did not exist. Rio Grande
LNG comes online in 2027 about 12 miles from Starbase, the FAA is opening rulemaking on
siting more spaceports, and the co-location problem is about to repeat. We build the
cross-facility quantitative risk model for that new asset class, and sell it to both
sides plus their insurers and regulators.

## Why it helps

Each operator today can answer "what happens if my facility has an event." Neither can
answer "what happens if *theirs* does," and neither regulator asks: FERC and PHMSA
govern the terminal, the FAA governs the launch site, and the boundary between them is
nobody's jurisdiction. Everyone in that gap is exposed and knows it.

## The deck, slide by slide

**1. Title.** Peninsula. *Cross-facility risk for co-located critical infrastructure.*

**2. Problem.** Quantitative risk assessment stops at the fence line. Co-location of
dissimilar high-consequence facilities creates hazard paths that neither operator's
model contains and neither regulator reviews.

**3. Why now.** Rio Grande LNG, roughly $12B and about 1,000 acres, targeted to operate
in 2027, about 12 miles from Brownsville and adjacent to an orbital launch site (S12,
grade A). Texas LNG is a second project in the same area. The FAA opened rulemaking on
siting new spaceports in August 2026 (S33, grade B). This pairing is not a one-off; it
is the beginning of a pattern.

**4. Product.** A cross-facility quantitative risk model delivered as software plus a
maintained model subscription, not as a consulting report: shared hazard paths, joint
consequence modelling, cross-operator emergency response, and cryogenic road transport
routing through populated corridors.

**5. Market.** Comparables are the process safety and QRA vendors, DNV and the
specialist consultancies. The new asset class is co-located launch and energy
infrastructure, which today is a handful of sites and, under the siting rulemaking,
a growing one. **State honestly that this is a small market today.**

**6. Business model.** Model build fee, then annual model maintenance as facilities
change. Sell to both operators, their insurers, and the authorities.

**7. Moat.** The first cross-facility model becomes the reference other parties argue
against. Regulatory acceptance compounds.

**8. Ask.** $500K, or preferably two paid pilot studies.

## Anticipated questions

**"This is consulting with extra steps."** The fairest attack, and the answer has to be
structural: the deliverable is a maintained model with a subscription, not a report;
revenue recurs because facilities change; and the second model costs far less than the
first because the hazard library carries over. If we cannot demonstrate that by the
second engagement, you are right and this should not be venture funded.

**"You need facility data from two operators who will not give it to you."** Correct,
and it is the hardest gate. Two routes: start from public filings, FERC, PHMSA and FAA
environmental documents, and build the model that is defensible without proprietary
data; or enter through the insurer, who can require the data neither operator will
volunteer. The tournament scored this idea's startability at 3 out of 10 for exactly
this reason and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

**"Why won't DNV just do this?"** They can and might. They are organised by industry
vertical, which is precisely why the cross-vertical gap exists. That is a real but
temporary advantage measured in a couple of years.

**"How big can this get?"** Small today. It scales only if co-location becomes common,
which is what the siting rulemaking suggests but does not guarantee. If you need a
billion-dollar outcome, this is not it.

## Kill criteria

Walk away if neither operator nor any insurer will engage after two quarters, or if a
defensible model cannot be built from public filings alone.

# Deck 3 — SARGO

> **We get paid to take the feedstock, and the contaminant everyone else treats as the obstacle is the product.**

## Elevator pitch

**One line:** The Gulf just took 5 million metric tons of sargassum in a single month,
Texas pays about $2.9 million a year to shovel it off the beach, and 30 km away there is
a launch site that runs on methane.

**Thirty seconds:** Sargassum is the rare feedstock with a negative acquisition cost:
municipalities already pay to remove it. It anaerobically digests into methane. The
reason nobody has built a real business on it is arsenic, which the seaweed
bioaccumulates and which poisons both the digester's performance and the digestate's
downstream use. Everybody treats that as the obstacle. We treat it as the moat: solve
contaminant separation and you own a fuel business and a remediation business at the
same time, in the one place on earth that has record blooms, LNG liquefaction
infrastructure and a methane-fuelled launch customer within one county.

## Why it helps

Three parties currently pay for the same problem and get nothing back: coastal
municipalities pay to landfill it, tourism loses revenue to it, and a launch operator
buys methane trucked in from elsewhere. One process addresses all three.

## The deck, slide by slide

**1. Title.** SARGO. *Negative-cost feedstock. Contaminant separation as the moat.*

**2. Problem, sized.** The Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt hit a record 37.5 million tons
in 2025 (S34, grade B). In June 2026 the Gulf of Mexico alone hit about 5 million metric
tons, nearly double its previous record, with the eastern Caribbean at about 9 million
(S34, grade B). Texas spends roughly $2.91 million a year on beach removal; Miami-Dade
has estimated about $35 million a year (S35, grade B). EPA and the Texas General Land
Office both run inundation programmes (S36, grade A).

**3. The economics nobody else gets.** Feedstock cost is **negative**. You are paid to
accept the input. That is the same structural advantage landfill-gas and dairy RNG
projects built businesses on, and it is rare.

**4. Why nobody has done it — say this before they ask.** Arsenic. Sargassum actively
bioaccumulates it, because its phosphate transporter confuses arsenic for phosphate.
Arsenic is elevated in fresh biomass and released during decomposition. Digestate
carries arsenic and zinc requiring treatment before agricultural use, and heavy metals
may cause toxicity that further reduces methanogenic performance (S27, grade A). Methane
yield is modest, around 92.62 mL CH4 per gram of volatile solids (S26, grade B). And
Caribbean and Mexican groups are already commercialising general sargassum valorisation
(S28, grade A), so the general idea is not even novel. **Only the contaminant-first
framing and the Texas propellant pairing are.**

**5. The thesis.** If arsenic is why the field is empty, then arsenic separation is the
company. Two products from one process: pipeline or propellant-grade biomethane, and a
remediation and clean-digestate stream that unlocks the agricultural market everyone
else's digestate is locked out of.

**6. Why here.** Record Gulf blooms, existing LNG liquefaction infrastructure within
the county, and a methane-consuming launch customer about 30 km from the beach (S12,
S13). No other coastline has all three.

**7. Business model.** Tipping fees on intake, offtake on biomethane, and licensing the
separation process. Three revenue lines, the first of which starts before the technology
is proven.

**8. Ask.** $1.5M seed, or $400K non-dilutive to answer one question: can arsenic be
separated at a cost that leaves the fuel economic. **That question is the whole company.**

## Anticipated questions

**"Isn't the yield too low to matter?"** Probably, on fuel alone, at published yields.
The model does not close on fuel revenue by itself, which is why tipping fees and the
remediation product are not garnish. If your thesis needs fuel-only economics, pass.

**"Arsenic sounds like a research programme, not a startup."** For the first 12 months
it is, which is why the preferred ask is non-dilutive. Fund the question, not the
factory. If arsenic separation does not work at cost, we will tell you and stop.

**"Caribbean groups are already doing this."** They are doing valorisation broadly
(S28). Nobody is doing contaminant separation as the primary product, and nobody is
pairing it to a propellant customer. If that turns out to be wrong, the differentiation
is gone and so is the investment case.

**"Seasonality?"** Blooms are seasonal and volatile between years, so intake and
storage economics are a real design constraint, not a footnote. 2026 came in second to
2025 rather than setting a new record (S34), which tells you the variance is large.

**"Regulatory path for arsenic-bearing waste?"** Unresolved and material. Handling and
disposal classification could change the economics substantially. It belongs in
diligence, and I am flagging it rather than waiting for you to find it.

## Kill criteria

Walk away if arsenic separation cost exceeds the combined tipping plus fuel plus
remediation revenue at pilot scale, or if the waste classification makes handling
uneconomic.

# Deck 4 — The Boom Map

> **A high school student builds the dataset a venture-funded company does not have yet.**

**Read the audience carefully.** For this deck "investors" are competition judges,
programme sponsors and mentors, and the currency is not equity. But there is a genuine
investor angle, and it is the strongest thing about this project: **The Boom Map is the
cheapest possible proof of Boom Baseline's core assumption.** For about $2,000 it tests
whether a distributed sensor network around a launch site produces a coherent,
publishable gradient. A venture investor should care about that, because it is a
seven-figure question being answered for four figures.

## Elevator pitch

**One line:** Every airport measures its noise. No spaceport does. I built the first one,
on my street.

**Thirty seconds:** I live in Port Isabel. When Starship launches, my house shakes, and
so do my neighbours'. Right now the only measurements anyone has are a few academic
studies and an argument in federal court. I built fifteen sensors, put them on my
neighbours' homes and my school with their written permission, and published the first
public map of what each launch does to each street. Every major airport has run this
kind of network for decades. Not one spaceport has. I made the first one.

## Why it helps

The community gets a number instead of an argument. The school district finds out what
its buildings actually take. Researchers get open data nobody else has. And the launch
operator gets something it currently lacks: an independent record it can point to when
it says a mitigation worked.

## The deck, slide by slide

**1. Title.** The Boom Map. *The first independent record of what a launch does to a
neighbourhood.* Photograph of a sensor on a real house.

**2. Why me.** I live here. My house is one of the data points. That is not a
qualification anyone can buy, and it is the reason the neighbours said yes.

**3. The gap.** Toronto Pearson runs 25 permanent certified noise terminals (S23).
Every major airport does something similar. Searching for the equivalent at any
spaceport turns up test labs and one-off academic studies, not a network (S24).

**4. What I built.** Fifteen nodes across Port Isabel and South Padre at different
distances from the pad, plus one in the school. Under $500 of hardware. Calibrated
against each other to a measured, documented tolerance.

**5. What I found.** The map, the gradient with distance, the raw data published
openly, and the method written so another student can repeat it.

**6. What I am careful about, and this is the important slide.** Written consent from
every homeowner before installation. No address is ever published; everything is
aggregated by street. These are not laboratory-grade instruments and I say so. And I
measure what **arrived** at a house. I make no claim about what caused any damage to
any house. That distinction is the difference between science and an accusation.

**7. What is next.** A full year of data, which nobody else on earth would have. Open
publication. And a build guide so students at other launch sites can run the same
network.

**8. What I need.** Mentorship on calibration methodology, an internship where I can
learn to do this properly, and about $2,000 to extend to 40 nodes and a year of
operation.

## Anticipated questions

**"Did you really do this yourself?"** Bring the build log, the calibration
spreadsheet, and the failures. Show a node that did not work and explain why. Nothing
proves authorship like a well-documented mistake.

**"Isn't this just anti-SpaceX?"** No, and here is the test: I am giving the same data
to everyone, including them, on the same terms. If they can show a mitigation reduced
overpressure on my street, my network is how they prove it. A community that trusts the
number is how launch cadence goes up, not down.

**"Your sensors aren't calibrated to standard."** Correct. They are consumer-grade,
cross-calibrated against each other to a documented tolerance, and I publish that
tolerance. They establish relative gradient and repeatability, not certified absolute
levels. That limitation is on my slide, not hidden in my notes.

**"What if there's no launch during your project window?"** Then my result is a
deployed, calibrated, documented network and a build guide, which is still a real
result, and I will present it that way rather than pretending otherwise.

**"How is this a business?"** For me right now it is not, and I would rather be honest
about that than dress up a science project. It is the beginning of the dataset a real
company would need, and if it turns into one, the year of data is what makes it
defensible.

**"What about your neighbours' privacy?"** Written consent before every install,
aggregation by street, never an address. I wrote that policy before I built the first
sensor.

## Runbook for the student

- **Consent before hardware.** Write the one-page consent form first. A judge notices.
- **Start on your own house.** No permission needed, and a working node is what convinces
  the first neighbour.
- **Eight sites is the floor.** Below that it is data points, not a map.
- **Publish the raw data.** Openness is the entire credibility of the project.
- **Same entry, three venues.** ExF Space Entrepreneur Summer Academy, the Conrad
  Challenge (ages 13-18) and the Diamond Challenge take this with no rework (S20, S29).
- **Never say your house was damaged by a launch.** Say what your sensor measured. Let
  the number speak.

# Part 5 — Questions every investor will ask all four

**"Why hasn't anyone done this?"** Have the specific answer per idea, and never say
"nobody thought of it." Boom Baseline: the precondition, launch cadence near people, is
new. Peninsula: the space is gated behind two operators' data. SARGO: arsenic. Naming
the real reason is what makes the whitespace credible instead of suspicious.

**"How do you know nobody is doing it?"** The honest answer: "We searched patents, SBIR
and STTR awards, trade press, FAA filings and county records, and found nothing. That is
a negative search result, not proof. If you know of someone, tell me now and it changes
my plan today." An investor who hears that respects it. One who hears "nobody is doing
it" assumes you did not look.

**"What is your unfair advantage?"** For three of these four the honest answer is
location and timing, not proprietary technology. Say it. Then say what converts it into
a durable one: the longitudinal record, the first regulator-accepted model, the
separation process.

**"What kills this?"** Have the kill criteria memorised. Founders who can state the
conditions under which they would stop are the ones investors believe about everything
else.

**"How much have you validated with customers?"** Currently: not enough. Do not
inflate it. Say what you have, say what the next 30 days buys, and offer to report back.

# Part 6 — Delivery runbook

**Before the room**
- Grade every number on your slides A, B or DERIVED. Remove every C. The AFRL programme
  figures and any prize amount from a prior cycle are C.
- Rehearse the weak slide until it is your calmest one.
- Bring one physical object. A sensor node in a plastic enclosure beats a rendering.
- Prepare the one-page leave-behind: elevator pitch, the ask, the kill criteria, contact.

**In the room**
- Lead with the gap, not the technology. "Every airport does this. No spaceport does."
- Name your hole by slide six at the latest. Do not let them find it at slide ten.
- When you do not know, say "I don't know, and here is how I would find out." It scores
  higher than a confident guess and it is the only answer that survives diligence.
- Never claim causation from measurement. Never claim a licence you have not signed.
  Never present a projection as a finding.

**After the room**
- Send the leave-behind within 24 hours with one new fact you did not have in the room.
- Log every question you could not answer. Those questions are your next milestones,
  and the pattern across several pitches is more useful than any single meeting.

**Never do these**
- Never build or imply a plaintiff-side positioning for Boom Baseline or The Boom Map.
  It destroys the product's value, which is that both sides accept the number.
- Never publish data identifying a specific property.
- Never let a minor near cryogens, rocket hardware, industrial property or restricted land.
- Never claim the whitespace is proven. It is a search result until someone disproves it.
