# GENERICPROMPT — ULTIMATE SOLUTION TOURNAMENT + AUTONOMOUS EXECUTION OS

SUBJECT: [INSERT SUBJECT]

## PRIME OBJECTIVE

Find, design, validate, and make executable the strongest solution reasonably discoverable for the SUBJECT.

Treat this as:

* an exhaustive competitive investigation;
* an architecture challenge;
* an adversarial tournament;
* a synthesis problem;
* and an implementation/execution problem.

Do not merely summarize existing approaches.

Determine what should actually be:

* built;
* adopted;
* combined;
* removed;
* redesigned;
* replaced;
* or invented.

Everything in this prompt is conditional on relevance.

Do not force irrelevant technologies, architectures, evaluation criteria, research areas, execution methods, or data structures merely because they are mentioned here.

The SUBJECT may be an application, repository, architecture, algorithm, workflow, feature, UI, agent system, business process, infrastructure system, data platform, research problem, or almost anything else.

Adapt aggressively.

Do not artificially restrict the search space.

---

# PART I — DISCOVER THE BEST SOLUTION

## 1. UNDERSTAND THE SUBJECT FIRST

Before judging solutions, determine:

* what the SUBJECT actually is;
* the true objective;
* existing architecture/state if applicable;
* constraints;
* invariants;
* assumptions;
* failure costs;
* important environments/use cases;
* what success actually means;
* what is negotiable;
* what should be challenged.

If a repository, PR, implementation, design, or existing plan is part of the SUBJECT, inspect the actual implementation when possible.

Do not blindly trust:

* documentation;
* plans;
* PR descriptions;
* comments;
* architecture diagrams;
* claims that something is complete.

Verify important claims against reality.

Do not anchor on the current design simply because work has already been invested in it.

---

# 2. SCOUT AGGRESSIVELY

Search broadly and deeply across whatever sources are relevant, including when useful:

* mainstream solutions;
* obscure solutions;
* GitHub;
* unpopular or emerging repositories;
* research papers;
* technical papers;
* forums;
* Reddit;
* YouTube;
* technical blogs;
* engineering posts;
* startups;
* commercial products;
* open-source projects;
* academic work;
* experimental implementations;
* abandoned projects containing useful ideas;
* adjacent industries;
* competing architectures;
* unconventional approaches;
* recent innovations;
* older approaches that may still outperform newer ones;
* combinations not commonly discussed.

Free-think beyond obvious search terms.

Search using:

* synonyms;
* adjacent concepts;
* underlying subproblems;
* competing schools of thought;
* alternative framings;
* ideas from unrelated domains that may transfer.

Do not assume:

* popularity means quality;
* newer means better;
* existing packaged solutions must win;
* better marketing means better engineering.

Scout substantially more than 50 candidates when the search space supports it.

Do not create filler to reach a number.

Weak, duplicate, irrelevant, obsolete, or clearly dominated candidates should be filtered before tournament entry.

---

# 3. SEARCH UNTIL DIMINISHING RETURNS

Do not stop because an arbitrary search count has been reached.

Continue scouting while new investigation produces materially different, tournament-worthy ideas.

Stop when additional exploration overwhelmingly produces:

* duplicates;
* weaker variants;
* already-covered concepts;
* irrelevant approaches;
* or no meaningful changes to the competitive field.

Record why scouting appears saturated.

# 5. TEST GENERIC VS SPECIALIZED

Explicitly investigate whether the strongest answer is:

* workload-specific;
* organization-specific;

* environment-specific;
* feature-specific;

Track where competitors win and where they lose.

Do not label something universally best merely because it dominates one environment.

# 6. BUILD THE TOURNAMENT FIELD

Select only serious competitors.

Aim for approximately 50 strong tournament entries when the subject supports that scale, but never manufacture weak entries.

Prefer materially different approaches over minor variations.

For each important competitor determine:

* core mechanism;
* architecture;
* intended environment;
* strengths;
* weaknesses;
* assumptions;
* dependencies;
* maturity;
* evidence;
* known failure modes;
* environments where it excels;
* environments where it struggles;
* what materially differentiates it.

Normalize duplicates where appropriate.

Preserve variants when their differences materially affect outcomes.

---

# 7. RUN A TRIPLE-ELIMINATION TOURNAMENT

Run a rigorous triple-elimination tournament.

A matchup is not a single simplistic comparison.

Treat each matchup as multiple battles across relevant:

* criteria;
* environments;
* workloads;
* difficulty levels;
* constraints;
* architectures;
* operational conditions.

When relevant include:

* easy;
* normal;
* difficult;
* adversarial;
* edge cases;
* low-resource;
* high-load;
* large-scale;
* legacy;
* greenfield;
* ambiguous requirements;
* changing requirements;
* partial failure;
* catastrophic failure.

Triple elimination exists so that a strong competitor is not removed because of one narrow weakness.

Track:

* overall performance;
* category performance;
* environment performance;
* difficulty performance;
* consistency;
* catastrophic weaknesses;
* confidence;
* evidence quality.

Allow decisive victories.

Do not manufacture artificial balance.

When evidence is weak, lower confidence rather than inventing certainty.

---

# 8. MAKE BATTLES ADVERSARIAL

For meaningful matchups determine:

* why one wins;
* why another loses;
* whether the loss is fundamental or fixable;
* conditions that reverse the result;
* hidden assumptions;
* tradeoffs;
* failure modes;
* downstream consequences;
* integration consequences;
* operational consequences;
* long-term consequences.

Attack your own conclusions.

Try to make leading candidates fail.

---

# 9. PRESERVE MULTI-DIMENSIONAL RESULTS

Do not collapse everything prematurely into one arbitrary score.

Maintain useful dimensions such as:

* raw category score;
* weighted score;
* battle record;
* environment record;
* difficulty record;
* elimination path;
* strengths;
* weaknesses;
* confidence;
* evidence quality.

An overall score may be produced afterward.

Never allow averages to hide fatal defects.

---

# 10. FIND COMPONENT-LEVEL WINNERS

Do not only determine which complete solution wins.

Determine which competitor has the strongest implementation of each meaningful component.

Preserve useful ideas from losing competitors.

A losing solution may contain the best:

* subsystem;
* algorithm;
* scheduling strategy;
* data structure;
* interface;
* workflow;
* validation mechanism;
* learning mechanism;
* memory system;
* security mechanism;
* orchestration strategy;
* observability design;
* UX concept.

These become raw material for synthesis.

---

# 11. BUILD FRANKENSTEIN / HYBRID CHALLENGERS

After the primary tournament, construct at least four serious hybrid challengers when meaningful.

Do not make superficial combinations.

Each hybrid must form a coherent architecture.

For each hybrid explain:

* borrowed components;
* source competitors;
* why they were selected;
* how they interact;
* incompatibilities that had to be resolved;
* weaknesses removed;
* new weaknesses introduced;
* implementation complexity;
* expected advantages;
* expected failure modes.

You may invent new components where necessary.

Do not assume hybrids automatically outperform originals.

---

# 12. RUN THE HYBRID TOURNAMENT

Test the hybrids rigorously.

Use harder tests where appropriate because the hybrids have already benefited from knowledge generated during the original tournament.

Evaluate:

* component interaction;
* architecture coherence;
* emergent failure modes;
* implementation reality;
* integration overhead;
* maintenance;
* scalability;
* adaptability;
* operational complexity.

Select the strongest hybrid.

---

# 13. FINAL CHAMPIONSHIP

Take:

1. the strongest original;
2. the strongest hybrid.

Run a serious final championship.

Attack both.

Use the hardest scenarios discovered.

Determine:

* absolute winner;
* why;
* remaining weaknesses;
* conditions where the runner-up remains preferable;
* confidence.

If neither should be adopted unchanged, design the superior final architecture.

The tournament is allowed to produce something that did not previously exist.

# PART II — TURN THE WINNER INTO AN EXECUTABLE SYSTEM

## 14. DO NOT STOP AT THE PLAN

Reverse-engineer the winner into an executable implementation system.

Do not merely create a large document describing work humans must manually coordinate.

The implementation plan must be accompanied by a repository-resident EXECUTION CONTROL PLANE.

The desired end state is:

PLAN ONCE
→ INSTALL CONTROL PLANE ONCE
→ MERGE BOOTSTRAP
→ USE ONE DISPATCHER FOREVER

The human operator should not have to remember:

* which node ran;
* which branch merged;
* which PR is open;
* which dependencies completed;
* which model was recommended;
* which integration wave is next;
* whether a repair is needed;
* whether the plan changed.

The system must reconstruct this.

---

# 15. SELECT THE EXECUTION STRUCTURE DYNAMICALLY

Do not automatically force breadth-first execution.

Choose whatever execution structure actually fits:

* dynamic dependency DAG;
* DAG;
* critical-path graph;
* state machine;
* pipeline;
* tree;
* graph;
* work queue;
* event-driven execution;
* staged rollout;
* recursive decomposition;
* breadth-first layers;
* depth-first branches;
* hybrid.

For complex software work, strongly prefer a dynamic dependency DAG unless another structure is clearly better.

The structure exists to improve execution.

It is not sacred.

---

# 16. DO NOT OVER-DECOMPOSE

Do not create thousands of tiny nodes merely to appear thorough.

A node should be large enough for a capable worker to accomplish meaningful independent work, but small enough to:

* validate independently;
* assign independently;
* recover independently;
* parallelize safely;
* understand its boundaries.

Prefer meaningful execution units.

---

# 17. DEFINE A MACHINE-READABLE NODE CONTRACT

Every executable node should define where relevant:

* immutable node ID;
* objective;
* rationale;
* dependencies;
* assumptions;
* required inputs;
* expected outputs;
* contracts/interfaces;
* read scope;
* intended write scope;
* forbidden scope;
* expected branch;
* acceptance criteria;
* required tests;
* evidence requirements;
* risk;
* reversibility;
* expected effort;
* critical-path importance;
* downstream unlock value;
* merge/conflict surface;
* file locks;
* semantic locks;
* parallel-safety information;
* rollback strategy;
* stopping condition;
* escalation conditions;
* minimum OpenAI model + reasoning effort;
* minimum Anthropic model + reasoning effort.

Workers should load their canonical node definition from the repository.

Do not require the human to paste the entire master plan into every worker chat.

---

# 18. OPTIMIZE EXECUTION CONTINUOUSLY

A node becomes executable as soon as its true dependencies are satisfied and execution is safe.

Do not wait for an artificial level to finish.

Prioritize dynamically using factors such as:

* critical path;
* downstream unlock value;
* blocker removal;
* uncertainty reduction;
* information gain;
* risk reduction;
* parallelization potential;
* merge-conflict risk;
* architectural importance;
* effort;
* worker capability;
* cost;
* reversibility;
* validation requirements.

Execute the maximum safe set of dependency-ready work concurrently.

Avoid concurrency that creates architectural divergence or unnecessary contention.

---

# 19. ALLOW THE GRAPH TO CHANGE

The plan is not immutable.

New evidence may expose:

* missing dependencies;
* wrong assumptions;
* unnecessary work;
* superior decomposition;
* hidden blockers;
* architectural conflicts;
* new parallelism;
* newly obsolete nodes;
* better implementation paths.

Nodes may be:

* added;
* removed;
* split;
* merged;
* reprioritized;
* invalidated;
* superseded;
* replaced.

Maintain traceability explaining why.

If implementation evidence disproves a tournament conclusion, reopen the decision.

Evidence outranks the original plan.

# PART III — AUTONOMOUS EXECUTION CONTROL PLANE

## 20. REPOSITORY STATE IS THE SOURCE OF TRUTH

Never depend on a manually maintained spreadsheet or manually edited status file as the authoritative execution state.

Every dispatcher invocation must reconstruct state from reality.

Inspect, where available:

1. exact current target-branch SHA;
2. commit ancestry;
3. validated completion receipts;
4. merged PR history;
5. open PRs;
6. draft PRs;
7. ready PRs;
8. closed-unmerged PRs;
9. remote node branches;
10. integration branches;
11. release branches;
12. CI/check status;
13. changed paths;
14. overlapping unrelated work;
15. integration receipts;
16. promotion receipts;
17. completion tags;
18. optional-trigger status;
19. plan fingerprint/version.

Never infer completion purely from:

* branch name;
* PR title;
* documentation;
* comments;
* stale status files.

A node counts as complete only when repository evidence proves that accepted work exists in the intended integration/target history.

Deleting a branch after merge must not erase completion evidence.

---

# 21. RECONCILE UNPLANNED WORK

Every dispatcher run must detect work that happened outside the expected execution flow.

Look for:

* new target-branch commits;
* unrelated merged PRs touching planned surfaces;
* unexpected open PRs;
* implementation completed under another PR;
* duplicate work;
* stale branches;
* conflicting migrations;
* changed interfaces;
* altered architectural assumptions;
* node work partially implemented elsewhere;
* a planned node that is now unnecessary.

Do not blindly launch stale instructions.

If reconciliation is needed, emit a RECONCILIATION action first.

---

# 22. EXECUTION STATE MACHINE

The controller should explicitly determine a state such as:

* BOOTSTRAP_REQUIRED
* RECONCILIATION_REQUIRED
* READY_TO_LAUNCH
* RUNNING
* WAITING
* BLOCKED
* REPAIR_REQUIRED
* ESCALATION_REQUIRED
* READY_TO_INTEGRATE
* INTEGRATING
* READY_TO_PROMOTE
* REPLAN_REQUIRED
* COMPLETE

Do not make the human infer the state.

---

# 23. ATOMIC NODE CLAIMING

Provide an atomic claim mechanism.

Before starting, a worker must claim its node.

The claim operation must verify:

* dependencies are still satisfied;
* the target branch has not invalidated assumptions;
* no valid existing claim exists;
* no conflicting PR appeared;
* no unsafe write-path or semantic contention exists.

Prefer a remote repository primitive such as an atomically created node branch as the distributed lock.

Do not rely only on local lock files.

---

# 24. NODE BRANCHES AND INTEGRATION WAVES

Each independent implementation node should normally execute on its own branch.

Implementation workers must not directly merge themselves into `main`, `staging`, production, or another protected target unless the generated governance policy explicitly allows it.

Do NOT force integration according to arbitrary BFS levels.

Instead create dynamic INTEGRATION WAVES / COHORTS based on:

* dependency readiness;
* architecture compatibility;
* write overlap;
* semantic overlap;
* migration ordering;
* risk;
* testability.

A typical pattern may be:

NODE BRANCHES
→ INTEGRATION WAVE BRANCH
→ optional RELEASE/CANDIDATE branch
→ independent PROMOTION
→ TARGET

Multiple nodes that compose naturally may share an integration wave even if they originated from different nominal depths in the DAG.

Do not merge independently green nodes directly to the target merely because each node passed alone.

Composition itself must be validated.

---

# 25. COMPLETION RECEIPTS

Every worker must create a machine-readable receipt containing at least:

* node ID;
* plan fingerprint;
* starting target SHA;
* final worker head SHA;
* branch;
* PR number/URL;
* paths changed;
* tests executed;
* results;
* acceptance evidence;
* discovered assumptions;
* deviations;
* unresolved risks;
* proposed follow-up work;
* status.

Receipts must be bound to the actual implementation being evaluated.

A stale receipt must not validate a newer commit.

---

# 26. INTEGRATION

When a safe integration cohort is ready, generate a dedicated integration action.

The integration worker must independently:

* inspect current target state;
* verify relevant node receipts;
* verify exact worker commits;
* reconcile conflicts;
* compose the changes;
* run cross-node validation;
* check architecture consistency;
* detect emergent failures;
* create an integration receipt;
* open the required PR;
* stop at the defined stopping condition.

Do not assume independent node success guarantees integration success.

---

# 27. INDEPENDENT PROMOTION

Where risk warrants it, promotion must be separate from implementation and integration.

The promotion worker must verify actual integrated code and evidence.

Do not trust worker summaries as proof.

Promotion should examine:

* CI;
* tests;
* receipts;
* target diff;
* migration ordering;
* security implications;
* unresolved risks;
* rollback readiness;
* required approvals.

---

# 28. REPAIR / FAILURE RECOVERY

Failures must become first-class states.

If a worker, integration, CI run, or reconciliation check fails:

* classify the failure;
* preserve evidence;
* determine whether the original node is still valid;
* produce the smallest safe repair action;
* decide whether the graph must change;
* route the repair to the appropriate model tier.

Do not force the human to diagnose which prompt comes next.

# PART IV — MINIMUM MODEL ROUTING

## 29. ROUTE EVERY NODE TO THE LOWEST SAFE MODEL

For every executable action determine:

> What is the lowest OpenAI model + reasoning effort and the lowest Anthropic model + reasoning effort that is reasonably safe for an independent first attempt?

Do NOT default to the strongest model.

Provide BOTH providers for every node/action:

OPENAI:

* minimum model;
* minimum reasoning effort.

ANTHROPIC:

* minimum model;
* minimum reasoning effort.

These are minimum safe first-attempt recommendations, not maximum-quality recommendations.

---

Assess at least:

* code complexity;

* number of systems involved;
* debugging difficulty;
* concurrency;
* security/privacy;
* authorization;
* database/schema impact;

Use a provider-neutral capability classification internally, for example:

---

# 31. MODEL ESCALATION

A worker must not continue blindly when the problem becomes materially harder than its assigned routing assumed.

Every node must define escalation conditions.

Examples:

* unexpected authentication redesign;
* database migration becomes necessary;
* security boundary changes;
* major architectural assumption fails;
* significantly wider write scope;
* acceptance criteria conflict;
* undocumented distributed/concurrency behavior appears;
* worker cannot prove correctness.

When triggered:

1. stop safely;
2. preserve evidence;
3. mark ESCALATION_REQUIRED;
4. recommend the new capability tier;
5. let the dispatcher generate the restart/repair prompt.

Do not preserve an outdated DAG because it was written earlier.

Do not over-decompose nodes.

Do not require humans to maintain state that software can derive.

Do not waste powerful models on deterministic bookkeeping.

Do not allow a cheap worker to silently exceed its risk envelope.

Do not let worker branches directly promote themselves into protected targets unless explicitly and safely authorized.

Continuously ask whether there is a stronger solution, simpler architecture, safer parallelization strategy, cheaper trustworthy model, or more autonomous way to execute.

The objective is not to produce a respectable plan.

The objective is to discover the strongest solution and package it as a self-reconciling execution system that can actually finish the work.
